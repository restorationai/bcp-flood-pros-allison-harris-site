## Who do you call first after storm damage, your insurer or a restoration company?

Call a restoration company first if the damage is active, meaning water is still coming in, a hole is open to the weather, or a window is blown out. Call your insurance company once a tarp or board-up is in place and you've taken photos, so the loss is already documented and being mitigated instead of deteriorating while you're on hold.

Most standard homeowners policies cover wind and hail damage as named perils, meaning a shingle blown off by wind or a window cracked by hail is typically a covered loss, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance). Flood water that enters from outside, like surface water from a flash-flood event or a canyon runoff, usually is not covered under a standard policy; that needs a separate flood policy. Homeowners also have a duty to mitigate further damage once a loss happens, which is one reason starting emergency tarping before the adjuster calls back is normal, not something that jeopardizes the claim.

## Who do you call first for safety hazards, power lines, gas, or structural damage?

Call 911 or your utility first for any downed power line, exposed wiring, or gas odor, before calling anyone about repairs. Rocky Mountain Power and Dominion Energy can respond to a hazard faster than any contractor, and no repair work should start until the property is confirmed safe.

That also covers anything that looks structurally unstable: a sagging ceiling, a roof section that's visibly pulled away from the frame, or a tree leaning on the house. Stay out of that part of the structure until it's been checked. If a storm watch or warning is active for Utah County, the [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/) is the source to check before you go back outside to assess anything.

## When do you need a roofer instead of a restoration company?

Call a [roofer](/services/roofing/) directly only if the damage is cosmetic and dry, like a few missing shingles with no sign of an interior leak. Call a restoration company first if water has already reached the attic, ceiling, or interior walls, since they coordinate the [emergency tarping](/services/emergency-board-up-tarping/), the drying, and the roof repair together instead of you managing two separate vendors on two separate timelines.

Utah County sees more than one kind of storm risk over a year: canyon east-wind events along the Wasatch Front, summer microbursts and hail, and monsoon-season flash flooding that can push water in through window wells and grade-level entries faster than a sump pump keeps up. As the Wasatch Front heads into winter, heavy wet snow loads and ice dams become the bigger concern, since trapped meltwater under an ice dam can work its way under shingles and into a ceiling cavity over days, not minutes. If that happens, you're looking at both a roof problem and a [water removal](/services/emergency-water-removal/) problem at the same time, which is exactly the scenario a restoration company is built to run.

## What should you document before cleanup starts?

Take photos and a slow video walkthrough of every damaged area before anything is moved, torn out, or dried. Capture the roof from the ground if it's safe to do so, the interior ceiling or wall where water entered, any standing water lines, and damaged contents still in place.

This matters because insurance adjusters work from documentation, not memory. A clear "before" record, paired with the restoration company's moisture readings and photos once drying starts, is what turns a disputed claim into an approved one. ## What happens after you call for storm damage help?

The first call is mostly information gathering: your address, what kind of damage it is, whether water is actively entering, and whether the power or gas has already been shut off if needed. Have your insurance policy number ready if you have it, but don't wait to find it before calling.

On arrival, the crew's first job is stopping the damage from getting worse: tarping the roof, boarding a broken window or door, and mapping how far the water traveled with a moisture meter. Photos get taken before any materials come out.
