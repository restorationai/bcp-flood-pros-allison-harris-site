## Is this a roofer's job or a water damage company's job?

It is both, and that is exactly why a one-call combination matters here. A roof leak after a storm needs someone to stop the water at the source, usually a tarp over the damaged section, and someone to dry out the insulation, drywall, and framing underneath before mold sets in.

Most roofers can tarp a roof and schedule a repair, but they are not equipped to run extraction equipment or dry a wet ceiling cavity to the IICRC [S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for water damage. Most water mitigation companies can dry a ceiling but cannot patch the roof deck itself. A company that offers both [roofing](/services/roofing/) and [water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/) can send one crew that tarps the opening, through [emergency board-up and tarping](/services/emergency-board-up-tarping/), and starts drying the same day, instead of you waiting on two separate schedules while water keeps spreading into the attic insulation.

There are two calls that come before this one. If a power line is down or sparking near the leak, call 911 or your utility first. If the roof structure looks like it is sagging or could collapse, stay out of that room and call for an assessment before anyone goes back in. Once the building is safe, make the restoration call.

## What should you do in the first 30 minutes?

Move fast but stay out of danger. Set a bucket or a tarp to catch the water, pull furniture and electronics out from under the drip, and if water is pooling near a ceiling light fixture, shut off power to that circuit at the breaker before it gets worse.

- Catch the water with a bucket, tub, or tarp, and poke a small relief hole in a bulging ceiling patch if water is pooling behind drywall, so it drains in a controlled spot instead of collapsing on its own.
- Cut power to any circuit near the leak if you are not certain it is safe.
- Photograph the ceiling, the water on the floor, and anything it touched before you move items or start cleanup, for the insurance file.
- Do not climb on a wet roof in storm conditions. Leave that to a crew with the right harness and footing.

## Will insurance cover a roof leak from a storm?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, storm-caused roof damage, like wind lifting shingles or hail puncturing the roof deck, but they exclude damage from gradual wear, like shingles that finally failed after decades of sun exposure. The insurer is looking for evidence the storm caused the opening, not years of deferred maintenance.

Document the storm date, the damage, and anything the crew finds once they open up the ceiling. Keeping receipts and photos protects the claim; see [what a standard homeowners policy typically covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) from the Insurance Information Institute. ## Does a roof leak cause mold?

Yes. Mold can start growing on wet insulation and drywall within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so the ceiling cavity needs to actually dry out, not just look dry on the surface.

A ceiling that looks dry to the touch can still hold wet insulation above it. That is why a moisture meter check matters more than a visual check. ## What about ice dam leaks in the winter?

Ice dams happen when heat escaping the attic melts snow on the upper roof, the meltwater refreezes at the cold eave, and the backed-up water works under the shingles into the ceiling. This is a common pattern along the Wasatch Front after a heavy, wet snow load.

Treat an ice dam leak the same way as a storm leak: catch the water, cut power to any nearby circuit, and call rather than chipping at the ice yourself with a hammer or ax, which usually damages the shingles further.
