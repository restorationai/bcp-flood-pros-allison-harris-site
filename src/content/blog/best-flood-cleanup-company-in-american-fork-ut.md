## What separates a flood cleanup company from a general water damage company?

Flood cleanup means moving standing water out of a structure fast, often from a source outside the home, like groundwater, a storm event, or a sewer backup, not just drying a wet carpet after a leaky washing machine hose. The equipment and the contamination risk are both bigger.

A company set up for flood work owns submersible trash pumps and truck-mounted extraction units that can pull hundreds of gallons an hour out of a basement, not a handful of portable extractors. They also carry enough air movers and commercial dehumidifiers to dry a finished basement, not a single bedroom.

## What equipment should a flood cleanup crew actually show up with?

At minimum, a flood crew should bring a gas or electric trash pump for standing water, a truck-mounted or portable extraction unit, low-grain-refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers, and enough axial air movers to cover the wet square footage, typically one per 10-16 feet of affected wall.

If a basement has more than an inch or two of standing water, a shop vac is not adequate equipment. Ask directly: "What pump and extraction equipment will be on the truck?" A company that can answer with specific equipment names, rather than "our standard setup," is one that actually owns the gear.

## How do you know a company can handle groundwater or sewage, not just a clean-water spill?

Flood water is rarely as clean as a burst supply line. Groundwater seeping through a foundation is considered Category 2 (gray) water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and any backup coming from a sewer line or floor drain is Category 3 (black water), regardless of how it smells or looks.

That distinction changes the job. Category 2 and 3 water usually means removing porous materials like carpet pad and baseboard rather than drying them in place, plus antimicrobial treatment and PPE for the crew. A company that treats every flood the same way, drying everything in place regardless of the source, is skipping a step that protects your health and your home.

Ask how they categorize the water before work starts and whether they test for contamination level. ## What questions should you ask before you hire anyone?

A short checklist you can run through on the phone, before a single truck leaves the lot:

- What pump and extraction equipment is on the truck right now?
- How do you categorize the water (clean, gray, or black) and what does that change about the plan?
- Is someone answering this line 24/7, or am I leaving a message?
- Do you work directly with my insurance adjuster, or do I handle that separately?
- Will I get a written scope of work before anything is removed or demolished?
- Are you licensed and insured in Utah?

A company that answers these without hedging has done this work before. One that gets vague about equipment or categorization is worth a second call before you commit.

## How does insurance billing and documentation work with a flood cleanup company?

Sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst pipe, is typically covered under a standard homeowners policy, but groundwater flooding and sump pump failures usually are not unless you carry a separate flood policy or a water backup endorsement, per the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance). That's worth knowing before you assume a flood loss is automatically covered.

Because coverage depends on the source of the water, documentation matters more in a flood claim than almost any other restoration job. A good flood cleanup company photographs the water line, logs moisture readings daily, and provides an itemized scope the adjuster can review.
