## Who do I call first when water is actively spreading through my home?

Call a water damage restoration company first if the water source is already stopped or is something you can shut off yourself, like a washing machine hose, a water heater, or a supply line under a sink. That call gets extraction equipment and dehumidifiers moving while there's still time to save drywall, subfloor, and trim.

The type of water matters for how fast you move. Clean supply-line water (Cat 1) stays manageable for a few hours. Water that's picked up contamination from a dishwasher or washing machine (Cat 2) should be treated faster. Sewage or flood water (Cat 3), also called black water, needs removal the same day, with the crew following the [ANSI/S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for how it's handled and what gets removed versus dried in place.

American Fork's lower benches, where a lot of finished basements sit close to the water table along American Fork Creek, tend to see slower-draining water sit longer against foundation walls. That's one more reason to make the call as soon as you spot standing water rather than waiting to see if it dries on its own.

## When should I call a plumber, electrician, or the utility company instead?

Call a plumber before a restoration crew if water is still flowing and you can't find or reach the main shutoff valve. A restoration crew can start extraction around an active leak, but someone needs to stop the source, and a licensed plumber does that faster than most homeowners can manage under pressure.

Call an electrician or your utility company, not a restoration crew, if standing water is anywhere near outlets, a sub-panel, or a furnace with exposed wiring. Walking through water near live electrical components is a real shock hazard. If there's any doubt, shut off power at the breaker from a dry spot, or wait for the utility company to confirm it's safe.

## What does a restoration crew actually do once they're on-site in American Fork?

A restoration crew's first job on arrival is extraction and moisture mapping, not demolition. They pull standing water with truck-mounted or portable extractors, then use a moisture meter and sometimes a thermal camera to find water that's already migrated into walls or under flooring where you can't see it.

From there, they set air movers and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers sized to the affected square footage, and they document moisture readings daily until materials dry to the S500 benchmark. That daily log is also what supports your insurance claim. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) notes that documented, dated evidence is one of the biggest factors in how smoothly a disaster claim settles, so a crew that photographs and logs as they go is doing you a favor beyond just the drying.

Once materials are dry, any affected drywall, flooring, or cabinetry that didn't survive gets scoped for repair, which is a separate phase from the emergency mitigation work.
