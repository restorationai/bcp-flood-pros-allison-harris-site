## What trauma scene cleanup actually involves

Trauma scene cleanup addresses the aftermath of a death, violent crime, or serious accident where blood, bodily fluids, or other biological material has affected a space. This is different from a routine cleaning job: materials that have absorbed fluids (carpet, padding, subflooring, drywall) often can't be sanitized and need to be removed, while surrounding surfaces require a documented decontamination process. Our technicians work in full protective equipment, follow a structured removal and disposal sequence, and coordinate with property owners, law enforcement, and sometimes funeral homes or landlords depending on the situation. Most residential jobs are completed in a single visit; larger or more affected areas may take longer.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and containment.** We identify every affected surface and material, not just what's visible at first glance, and isolate the work area from the rest of the property.
2. **PPE and equipment staging.** Technicians suit up in protective gear appropriate to the scope of contamination before any material is touched.
3. **Removal of non-restorable materials.** Carpet, padding, and other porous materials that have absorbed biological fluid are removed and bagged according to biohazard waste handling practices; they cannot be cleaned and reused.
4. **Decontamination and treatment.** Remaining hard surfaces are cleaned, disinfected, and treated with enzymatic or antimicrobial products to address residue and odor at the source.
5. **Disposal documentation.** Biohazard waste is logged and removed through a regulated disposal process, and we provide documentation for the property owner's records.

## What separates a good trauma cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is surface-level cleaning that leaves contamination in subflooring, wall cavities, or HVAC pathways. Blood and fluids travel through grout lines, along baseboards, and under flooring seams faster than most people expect, and a crew that only wipes down visible surfaces will leave a property with lingering odor and a biohazard risk that resurfaces later. A thorough response means checking adjacent rooms, testing for fluid migration under flooring, and being willing to remove more material than seems necessary rather than less. Insurance adjusters reviewing these claims typically want photo documentation of the affected area before and after treatment, an itemized list of what was removed and why, and proof of proper disposal, which is why we document each step rather than treating it as a quick cleanup.
